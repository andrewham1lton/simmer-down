How a phone camera reads your pulse through a fingertip

Cover your iPhone's rear camera and torch with a fingertip, turn the torch on, and the camera's picture glows red. That red light has travelled through your skin. Each heartbeat pushes a little more blood into your fingertip, and blood absorbs light, so the picture dims very slightly with every beat and brightens again between beats. Software follows that flicker and finds each beat.

The method is called photoplethysmography, or PPG for short. Many watches and fitness bands use the same idea, with their own small lights on the back.

Why the torch is on

The camera needs bright, unchanging light behind the skin. The torch sits next to the lenses. Its light spreads through your fingertip, and some of it comes back out over the lens. Room light is too dim to shine through a fingertip, and it changes as you move, which would hide the beats.

Why the picture is red

Red light travels further through skin than green or blue, so most of what reaches the lens is red. The change with each beat is far too small to see with your eyes. Software picks it up by comparing the brightness of the picture many times a second.

Why stillness matters

A heartbeat changes the brightness only a little. Moving your finger, or the phone, changes it far more and drowns out the beats. Rest your hand and the phone on a table, your lap or the arm of a chair, and keep the same touch for the whole session.

Why light pressure matters

Pressing hard squeezes blood out of your fingertip, and the flicker flattens out. Rest the pad of your finger lightly, with just enough contact to cover the lens and the light. If the reading drops out, ease off a little before you try anything else.

Other things that help

  • Warm hands. Cold narrows the small blood vessels in your fingertips and weakens the flicker. Rub your hands together or hold a warm mug first.
  • Cover the lens fully. Light leaking in around your finger adds its own flicker.
  • The right lens. On iPhones with more than one rear camera, the one nearest the torch usually gets the most light.
  • Breaks. The torch warms the back of the phone. Take a break between sessions if it feels too warm.

What a camera reading is for

A fingertip reading on a phone is a guide for breathing practice. It can drop out when you move, and it's a measurement you shouldn't rely on for anything else. To see what it shows during slow breathing, read why your heart rate rises when you breathe in.

Where Simmer Down fits

Simmer Down reads your pulse this way. It waits until a few beats in a row agree before it shows a number, and if your iPhone can't find your pulse, it offers to carry on hands-free.

The fingertip reading has been checked on iPhone 15 Pro Max. The camera picture is read on your iPhone as it comes in and is never saved or sent.
