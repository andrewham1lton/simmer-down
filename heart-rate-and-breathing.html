Why your heart rate rises when you breathe in

Rest two fingers on your wrist or the side of your neck and breathe in and out slowly. Many people can feel their pulse quicken a little as they breathe in and slow as they breathe out. That's normal and happens in almost everyone. Research describes it as larger in younger people and during slow breathing.

Why it happens

Your heart has a built-in brake: nerve signals, mostly carried by the vagus nerve, that slow it down. As you breathe in, that brake eases off for a moment, so your heart beats a little faster. As you breathe out, it comes back on, and your heart slows. The result is a small wave in your heart rate with every breath.

How it relates to HRV

Your heart doesn't beat like a metronome. The time between beats changes from one beat to the next, and heart rate variability, or HRV, is the name for that change. The wave that follows your breath is one of the main reasons for it when you're at rest.

Why slow breathing makes the wave larger

At a usual pace of 12 to 15 breaths a minute, your heart rate has little time to rise before the out-breath pulls it down again. Slow the breath and it has time to climb and fall fully. Research on paced breathing describes the wave as largest, for many adults, at around six breaths a minute, where it lines up with a slower rhythm the body already has. That's the idea behind resonance breathing.

How to see it for yourself

  • By feel. Keep your fingers on your pulse and breathe at six a minute for a minute or two.
  • With a sensor that shows each beat. A chest strap or a watch with a beat-by-beat view shows the wave as a line.
  • With a phone camera. A fingertip over the camera and torch can show it too. How that works.

Why it changes from day to day

Tiredness, caffeine, a hard workout, a late meal and how you're sitting all change the size of the wave. So it makes sense to compare a session with your own recent sessions, never with someone else's.
